Value is how light or dark something is, ignoring its color. Six items: sort three gray ladders light → dark (tap two chips to swap them), then three times make a gray that is exactly as light as a color — with the slider, or switch to the hatch pad and scribble until the paper holds the same tone.

Ladders score by how far the order beats a random shuffle (a perfect run is 100, the deal you start from is ~0); matches by how close your gray lands, out of 100 steps from black to white. Land within about 2 steps and you are matching by eye as well as a working painter does.

The hatch pad: the nib is fat and translucent, so five to ten passes take you across the whole range — there is no endurance test here. There is an eraser and an undo, and “clear” starts the pad over. Because the tone comes out of your hand rather than your eye alone, the hatch window is a little wider on a mouse or a finger than on a pen — a few L* of hand allowance, not a different drill; the slider scores the same on every device if you would rather not draw at all. A pad you have laid no ink on is not an answer and cannot be locked in: bare paper is the paper, not a value.

Studio tip: half-shut your eyes — detail drops away and only the light-and-dark pattern remains; that pattern is what makes a drawing read.

Judging lightness with the color squinted away

A round is six items and takes about 45 seconds. Three of them are gray ladders dealt out of order: tap two chips to swap them until the run reads light to dark, and the ranges narrow as you go. The other three show a colored swatch and ask for a gray of exactly its value — set it on the slider, or move to the hatch pad and lay strokes on white paper until the density of the marks carries the same tone. Saturation falls across those three, so the near-grays come last. Every item reveals the ideal answer over your attempt and then waits; nothing advances on its own.

The two halves of the round split the skill: sorting is reading lightness with nothing else in the frame, and matching is reading it while a colour sits next to it arguing. Sorting and matching with the color deliberately set aside is how an eye learns to read lightness by itself. The hatch pad matters for a second reason: judging a value and producing it with marks are separate skills, and both are measured here against the same ruler.

How a ladder and a match are marked

Ladders score on Kendall tau across the 15 chip pairs — 100 · max(0, 2 · ordered / 15 − 1) — so a perfect run is 100, a reversed one 0, and the deal you start from is worth roughly nothing. Matches convert both swatches to CIE L* through relative luminance, the hatch pad averaging its pixels in linear light the way squinting really blurs them, and then score 100 · (1 − ΔL* / 22), with the first 1 L* forgiven for slider granularity. Hatching is a drawn mark, so its window is eased per hardware — 22 on a pen, 27 on a finger, 32 on a trackpad — while the slider, pure eye, reads the same everywhere. The round is the mean of the six.
