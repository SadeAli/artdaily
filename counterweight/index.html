Each frame already holds some shapes. One more is waiting under the frame — press inside the frame where it belongs, drag to adjust, let go to place it. Four frames, then a score.

A shape pulls on the picture by its weight — how big it is times how strongly it reads against the paper — and by its arm, how far it sits from the middle of the frame. The frame balances when the pulls cancel out, so a small pale shape has to sit further out than a big dark one. That is the whole rule, and it is the whole score: closer to the balancing spot is more, and the score fades to 0 the further out you land.

Every frame ends with the answer drawn over your attempt: a ring on the spot that balances, a line from your shape to it, and the thin lever line — the middle of the frame is the pivot, and everything already in the picture pulls from the small dot at the other end of it. The dotted circle is where the score runs out; land on it and that frame is worth nothing.

At the end of the round, if every shape landed the same side of the mark, it says so — and how far the other way to go next round. Beginners nearly always place the counterweight too close to the middle; that habit is the thing to fix, not any one frame.

Studio tip: squint. Balance is a judgement about big masses and how dark they read, and squinting throws away the detail that is not part of it.

And a caveat worth having: a picture that balances perfectly can still be dull, and plenty of good ones lean on purpose. Learn where the pivot is first — then lean off it because you meant to.

Making a picture stop leaning

Every frame opens holding one to three flat masses, all leaning it one way, with one more shape waiting in the tray underneath. Press inside the frame where that shape belongs, drag to adjust, let go to place it. Four frames, then a score. Each frame ends with the answer drawn over your attempt: the balancing spot ringed, the gap dashed across to your shape, and the lever — a line from the pull of everything already in the picture, through the pivot at the centre, out to the answer.

Two things decide a shape's pull: its weight — area multiplied by how dark it reads against the paper — and its arm, the distance out from the frame's middle. Beginners count the weight and forget the arm, so the counterweight goes far too close to the middle and the picture keeps leaning; naming that habit is what the round-end line is for. Where the subject goes is a different question, one that cropping and thirds anchors answer; this is about what holds it up.

Balance is a moment sum, not a matter of taste

Take weight as area times darkness and the pivot as the middle of the frame, and the pulls of everything already there cancel at exactly one spot. The frame generator runs the same function the scorer uses, so the answer it validates is the answer it grades. No AI is involved: that spot is one line of algebra — the moments about the centre have to cancel — solved for where the missing shape must go. Your score is how far you landed from it, run against a zero-point set at 26% of the frame's width — a composition is judged in fractions of the picture, never in pixels of screen — with a floor in real pixels so a small sheet cannot turn hand tremor into the whole result. On a 600px sheet, 2% of the frame width out scores 92, 10% scores 62, 20% scores 23.
