match this grey’s value — how light it is

$ man value-trap

Value means how light or dark something is, ignoring its colour. One grey target, six loud colours — tap the colour that is exactly as light as the grey. Keys 1–6 work too; with a mouse, each chip wears its number.

The right chip scores 100; a miss still scores by how close its value was. The window is measured against that item's own gap, so the nearest wrong chip is always worth a visible handful of points whether the item is easy or hard. Six items per round, and the traps get subtler as you go.

After each pick every chip flips to the grey of its own value with its number on it — the squint view, the same thing your eye does when you half-shut it and the colours drop away. It stays up until you say so — “next”, or a tap anywhere on the board, chips included, or Enter — and the last one stays after the round ends. That number is L*: 0 is black, 100 is white. Labels are measured from the exact pixels shown, so the winner can sit a rounding hair off the target's number.

Studio tip: half-shut your eyes, or imagine the picture in black and white — the colour is shouting, the value is whispering, and it is the value that makes a painting read.

Seeing how light a colour is, not which colour it is

An item is one grey target chip, somewhere between L* 30 and 75, and six chips in loud hues. Exactly one of the six carries the grey’s value; tap it, or press its number, since the chips are keyed 1–6. The moment you pick, all of them flip to their own greyscale equivalents with their L* numbers printed on — the squint view — which stays up until you dismiss it, with no clock running, so a miss can be studied. Six items make a round, and the margin between the right answer and its nearest distractor tightens from 12 L* to 5.

Hue interferes with value, and that interference is the trap. A saturated chip announces itself, while its lightness — the property that decides whether a picture reads at all — says very little out loud. Half-shutting your eyes is the old remedy, and the squint view is that gesture made literal, but it arrives only once the answer is locked in, so the judgement itself happens with the colour still shouting.

What a near miss is worth

The right chip is 100. A wrong one still earns for being close in value: 38 × (1 − |ΔL*| / reach)³, where reach is 3.8× the tightest gap available on that item. Measuring against the item’s own gap keeps the nearest wrong chip worth a visible ~15 whether the item was easy or subtle, while distant picks fade to nothing. Cubing the fade is deliberate: with six chips and one right answer, guessing alone already banks about 17, and cubing holds a blind round close to that floor — a measured mean of 24 over 20,000 simulated rounds. A round is the mean of its six items.
