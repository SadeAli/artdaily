Drag the three sliders — hue (which color), saturation (how strong) and lightness (how light) — until your mix melts into the target, then lock it in. No numbers until you commit: this is an eye drill. The ± buttons step by exactly 1 — hold one down and it runs, the same way holding an arrow key on a focused slider does. On a phone that is how you land an exact hue: the track only has room for about 2° a pixel there, so the last few degrees belong to the buttons.

Scored by ΔE — how far apart your eye reads the two colors. Under 2 you cannot tell them apart, and that scores a full 100. The window scales with the target, so being 10° off costs about the same on a vivid color as on a grey one; what changes across the round is how hard the target is to read. Five colors per round: an easy warm-up, then vivid, then muted, ending near-grey — the hardest to judge. After the fifth, a recap lines up all five pairs.

Studio tip: get the lightness right first (half-shut your eyes until the colors drop away), then which color it is, then how strong. Most mixes fail on lightness, not hue.

Matching a color without numbers

Five target swatches a round, three sliders — hue, saturation, lightness — and no numbers until you commit. You push the mix until it melts into the target and lock it in; only then does the reveal show both HSL triples, how far apart they were, and which axis carried the miss — “biggest miss: lightness — yours lighter by 8”. The five run easy, then vivid, then muted, ending near-grey where the eye drifts furthest and the hint warns that hue has stopped mattering. A recap then lines all five pairs up with their scores.

Mixing well means splitting one impression into three judgements, and the reveal says which of the three you got wrong — most mixes fail on lightness, not hue. Nothing here is eased for hardware, because nothing here is a stroke: the eye that reads a colour is the same eye behind a pen, a trackpad or a thumb. What hardware does get is a way to enter the value you meant — ±1 steppers on both sides of each slider, plus the arrow keys, so an exact hue is reachable even on a phone track carrying a couple of degrees per pixel.

What ΔE measures here

Both colors are converted from HSL through sRGB, linear RGB and XYZ into Lab, then compared with CIE76 ΔE. That conversion is the whole judge — a formula, not a model, so the same pair of colors always scores the same number. A ΔE of 2 or under is below what the eye can pick apart and takes a full 100. Above that the score falls in a straight line to zero, and the zero point scales with the target's own chroma: about ΔE 45 on a vivid color, about 20 on a near-grey. That is what keeps a 10° hue error costing about the same wherever it lands. The first two items carry a little extra room, so the warm-up is a real win. The round is the mean of the five.
