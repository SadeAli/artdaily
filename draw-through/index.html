You may lift. Three laps is a long way to drag and a trackpad simply runs out of room: press again near where you stopped and the same loop carries on. Press somewhere else instead and you are starting that loop over, which costs nothing.

Scored against yourself. 75 of the 100 is how tightly your three laps agree — compared direction by direction, so a fast lap and a slow one are judged on the same geometry and speed is never what is scored. The other 25 is drawing roughly the shape asked for. Three confident laps of a slightly wrong ellipse beat three timid different ones: that is the whole lesson.

The reveal keeps your three laps as you drew them, prints the bold line they average out to, and puts a dotted pair either side of it — that corridor is where laps stop scoring. The tolerance is eased for whatever you are drawing with; the corner of the HUD says which. (The drill says this once, on your first reveal; it is here for the second time you wonder.)

Each ellipse is a real circle seen at an angle: the further it is turned away from you, the slimmer it gets, and the harder its laps are to keep together. The four in a round turn further away as you go.

If your laps grew — or shrank — the same way all round, the last line of the round says so. That spiral is the habit to fix, not any one loop.

Studio tip: ghost it first — two or three passes in the air, or with the button up, at the speed you mean to draw — then commit without slowing down. Laps stack when the arm is already moving and scatter when the hand starts steering.

Going round an ellipse until the laps agree

Each sheet holds one dashed ellipse with a dot at its centre. Press anywhere on it and go round three times in a single go; three pips at the foot of the sheet fill as the laps land, and the loop scores itself the moment the third one does. Lifting costs nothing: press again near where you stopped and the same loop carries on. Four ellipses per round, each a real circle turned further away from you than the last, roughly 31°, 46°, 57° and 64°.

Every beginner is told to go round an ellipse two or three times without stopping, and until this one no drill in the arcade ever checked whether those laps landed on each other. Repeatability is a different skill from accuracy, and the one that decides whether an ellipse looks drawn or fought. So agreement is worth 75 of the 100 here, and the shape itself 25.

How the three laps are compared

Your stroke is unwrapped into an angle around the guide’s centre, so a lap is one full turn and the count can run while you are still drawing. Each lap is then read off at the same 64 fixed directions, which is what makes two laps comparable at all: hurry one and dawdle over the next and the radii still match, so the number describes the curve and never the pace. Agreement is the mean spread of those radii around the laps’ own average, drift is the last lap minus the first — positive for a loop that grows, negative for one that tightens — and fit measures the mean lap against the guide. Agreement runs out at a tolerance eased for the device: 27.3px on a trackpad, 20.5px on a finger, 13.7px on a pen for a desktop-sized loop, scaled only weakly by the guide’s size so a phone’s smaller ellipse is not left with half the room for the same tremor.
