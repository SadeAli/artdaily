Three panels a round (two on your first ever round). Hatch inside the paper box — many short parallel strokes, packed tighter where the reference strip beside it is darker. Press done once the ramp reads (15 strokes is the minimum; 40 makes it smooth). Undo and clear cost nothing. “New round” asks before it throws live hatching away.

Scored on the shape of your ramp, never on how hard you press: the panel is measured in 24 bands of ink density, normalised, and compared with the target — plus a small bonus for keeping the strokes parallel. The reveal graphs your ramp against the target and re-draws your panel as flat bands.

Studio tip: keep every stroke the same length and the same angle, and let spacing do the work — a smooth ramp is a spacing problem, not a pressure problem.

Making a gradient by hand

Three panels make a round, about three minutes. Each is a sheet of paper with the target ramp printed on a swatch strip beside it, and you hatch inside the paper: short parallel strokes, packed tighter wherever the strip is darker. Ink accumulates live, so overlapping strokes really do darken. Panel one is a wide light-to-dark ramp, panel two a tall one running top to bottom, panel three a narrow two-stop shape — up to a mid tone, hold it, into the core dark, then a light band at the end. Fifteen strokes is the floor; undo and clear cost nothing.

For most people this is the first gradient built with a hand rather than a slider, and the error it corrects is going dark too fast. One idea holds the drill together: hold length and angle steady from stroke to stroke, and let the distance you leave between them decide the value. Over-hatching gets no special penalty — it saturates the ink and flattens the top of your ramp. After each panel the sheet is redrawn as flat bands with your hatching ghosting through, and your density curve is graphed against the target with the miss shaded between them.

How 24 bands become a score

The finished panel is read back in 24 bands along the ramp axis — mean ink density per band, taken from the alpha channel, so the paper and pencil colours of your theme cannot shift the number. Your curve and the target get the same small blur, a stand-in for squinting, and yours is divided by its own darkest band, so a light hand and a heavy hand score the same shape. That ramp score is 85% of the panel; the rest is parallelism, read from the doubled principal angle of each stroke, since hatching has no head or tail. The round is the mean of the three.
