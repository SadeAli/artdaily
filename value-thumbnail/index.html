Squint at the scene, then rebuild its value pattern. The grid starts all mid — pick a brush swatch and drag to paint cells, or tap a single cell to cycle it light → mid → dark. Picking a value is the swatches' job, so a tap never changes what your brush is loaded with, and lifting mid-drag to re-place your hand (which a trackpad has to do) carries on the same stroke instead of flipping a cell. “brush mode” swaps the grid for a freehand canvas: paint the masses in strokes and “done” downsamples them onto the same 12×8 grid for scoring. The two are the same answer seen two ways, so switching between them carries your work across.

“done” checks every cell against the scene’s own three-value map — its natural light, mid and dark groups (relative to this scene, not fixed cutoffs). Full credit exact, a sliver for one step off, nothing for flipped; borderline cells sitting right on a cut count either neighbouring value (dotted marks). A flat one-value grid scores 30 — the meter starts where observation begins.

After scoring, tap the scene or the grid to flip between your read and the true pattern.

Studio tip: paint the pattern, not the objects — three values is a composition tool, not a colouring book.

Reducing a scene to light, mid and dark

Each round paints a fresh procedural landscape and puts an empty 12×8 grid beside it, every cell starting on mid. You rebuild its value pattern in three flat values: pick a swatch and drag to lay a mass down, or tap a single cell to cycle it light → mid → dark. “Brush mode” hands you a freehand pad instead of the grid: lay the masses in as strokes, and pressing “done” reduces what you painted to the same 96 cells before scoring. Both surfaces edit one answer, so you can move between them without losing work.

Squinting is the thing being practised, and it is not a metaphor here: three values is barely enough resolution to describe an object, so the only way to fill the grid is to look at masses. A sky and a distant hill may collapse into one shape; a lit roof may belong with the water rather than with the wall under it. That is the read a thumbnail exists to test before any drawing is committed to — the pattern, not the objects.

Where the three cuts come from

The scene is drawn to an offscreen canvas, each of the 96 cells is averaged into a single luminance, and the light, mid and dark bands are then found by natural-breaks clustering of those 96 means (exact Fisher–Jenks, k = 3). The cuts belong to that scene, so a smooth gradient is not chopped at arbitrary thirds. An exact cell scores 1, one step off 0.15, a flipped cell 0, and cells sitting within a hair of a cut take either neighbouring value for full credit. The mean is rescaled against the best flat one-value grid, which lands on 30: perfect is 100, and painting nothing cannot beat 30. The reveal then overlays the true map — dashed one off, solid flipped, dotted borderline. That is a cell-by-cell comparison against a measurement of the same scene — no model reads your grid, and a given scene always cuts at the same two thresholds.
