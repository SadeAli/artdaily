drag the box until it matches the pose in the target panel — sideways spins it (yaw), up and down tips it (pitch). the painted dot tells you which face is which: no dot in the target means you are seeing its back, so spin past 90°. lock it in; five boxes per round.

on a trackpad: a slow drag stays fine (half a degree per pixel) while a fast one accelerates, so a 170° turn is one swipe rather than three. hold shift while dragging to spin only, alt to tip only — one axis at a time, which is how you stop fixing one and wrecking the other.

score is the pure rotation gap between your pose and the target: 0° off = 100, 40° off = 0. round score is the mean of five. arrow keys nudge 1° (shift = 5°), enter or space locks — and every lock snaps to whole degrees, so an exact 100 is reachable by drag too.

the first two boxes of your first round show the target ghosted faintly over your own, so you can see what “matching” looks like. after that it is from the panel, which is the actual skill.

after each lock the reveal names the fix in words — “spin 5° right, tip 4° up” — with the target ghosted over your box.

studio tip: match one axis at a time — line up the long edge first, then roll the dot face into place.

Turning a form in your head

A small panel holds an elongated box — proportions 2:1:1.4, one face marked with a painted dot — at a yaw and pitch you are never told. The larger box beside it starts elsewhere, and you drag until the two poses agree: sideways spins it, up and down tips it. A round runs five boxes, opening near the canonical views and finishing on rear-quarter tilts as steep as 72°. A target with no dot anywhere means you are seeing the back of the box, so the spin has to carry past 90°.

The box is a solid under a pinhole camera, not a flat diagram: the eye sits nine half-extents back, so the projection spreads about 1.34× across the form and at a three-quarter view the near vertical edge comes out roughly 30% longer than its far twin. The pose has to be read from that convergence and from which face carries the dot, never from the outline — turn a cuboid 180° about its own axis and its vertices land back on themselves, so two poses draw the identical silhouette. The same read decides whether a box, a head or a chair can be drawn from an angle you have never seen it at.

How the gap becomes a number

One number is measured and the route you took to it is not: the size of the single turn that would still carry your box onto the target's pose. Zero degrees off scores 100, forty degrees off scores 0, and the round score is the mean of the five. Every lock snaps your pose to whole degrees and every target is a whole degree, so an exact 100 is reachable by dragging, not only by arrow key. After the lock the target ghosts over your box and the reveal names the correction in words — off by 7.2°, spin 5° right, tip 4° up.
