A ball stands under a sky. Drag the rail until the ball's shadow side is the colour that sky would make it, then press “paint it”. Four balls, then a score.

Outdoors there are two lights, not one: the sun, and the whole sky. The sun makes the light side; the sky fills the shadow side. So a shadow is never just “the same colour, darker” — it carries the sky's colour, filtered through the object's own.

Scored on the colour alone: exactly right is 100, and the score fades to 0 as you drift warm or cool. The dotted band over the rail in the reveal is where it runs out.

After every ball the reveal paints two of them side by side — yours and the sky's — with both settings marked on the rail. At the end of the round it says which way you leaned, because that habit is the thing worth fixing.

Studio tip: the cast shadow on the ground is lit by the sky alone — no sun reaches it. Read its colour, then imagine that light landing on the ball instead of the ground.

Keyboard: tab to the sheet, then ← → to walk the rail (hold shift for bigger steps) and Enter to paint it.

Why a shadow is not the same colour, darker

Four balls, each under its own sky. The rail beneath runs from deep blue through colourless to low amber, and where you leave it is your answer for the colour of that ball's shade — press “paint it” to commit. Each ball ends in a reveal: yours and the sky's painted side by side, both settings marked on the rail with the gap drawn, and the miss put in words — a hair, a little, a good way, a long way too warm.

Outdoors there are two lights rather than one. The sun makes the light side; the whole sky fills the shade, which is why a shadow carries the sky's colour filtered through the object's own. Blue sky on a grey ball gives a blue-grey shade; blue sky on an ochre ball gives a grey-green one, and that second step is the one nobody takes unprompted. Which way to go is readable on the sheet: the cast shadow on the ground is lit by that sky alone, no sun in it. Every rail setting carries the same luminance, so the answer can never be found by value.

How far off the temperature was

The target is the scene's own fill temperature: exact by construction, not curated by hand. No model looks at the ball: the score is one subtraction on that rail. Your error is the distance to it along a rail running −1, deep blue, through 0 to +1, low amber. Exactly right is 100, fading linearly to 0 at a judgement tolerance of 0.62; only the hand's share of that is eased for hardware, so a trackpad adds 0.08 of slack, a finger 0.04 and a pen none. A perfect round is exactly 100, and if all four shades leaned the same way the closing line says so and how far to come back.
