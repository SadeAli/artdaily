$ man lines

Press down on the A dot, pull one confident stroke to B, lift. Six strokes per round — they get longer as you go. The reveal holds a moment so you can study it; a quick tap jumps to the next pair, and a press you keep down holds the reveal for as long as your finger does — the pause is a floor, not a clock.

You do not have to hit A exactly. Press anywhere inside the wide dashed circle and the drill measures your line from wherever you landed — a miss is never refused and never costs you.

Ran out of desk or trackpad halfway? Lifting early does not score. The drill says how far you got and draws a small circle where you stopped: press inside it and the same stroke carries on. Only a stroke that reaches B is graded.

Scored on how little your line curves away from the straight path between the two dots, minus a nick for stopping short of B — a visibly clean stroke earns a 100 on any device. The reveal marks where you drifted widest and reads your pull as steady or hesitant; your last two attempts stay faintly behind the new one with their straight paths, which is the studio technique. At round end you get your tendency: if most lines curve the same way, the drill says so.

The drill grades against the hardware it detects (shown in the corner as “scoring for …”), because the same wobble means different things from a mouse, a finger and a pen. Your score is only ever compared with your own.

Tip, pen or stylus: draw from the shoulder, not the wrist, and keep your eyes on the B dot — never on the tip. Tip, mouse or trackpad: one quick confident flick beats a slow careful drag — speed hides the wobble.

Pulling a line that does not wobble

Two dots, A and B. Press on A, pull one stroke across to B, lift. Six of them make a round, roughly forty seconds, and the pairs sit further apart as it goes on. Hitting A exactly is not part of it — a press anywhere inside the wide dashed circle is accepted and the line measured from where you actually landed. Your previous two attempts stay faintly on the sheet behind the new one, straight paths and all: redrawing over your own last try is the studio warm-up the whole drill is built out of.

What gets trained is the committed pull rather than the careful steer. The reveal overlays the true path, ticks the point of widest drift, and reads your stroke as a steady pull or as hesitant — a coaching note, never a penalty. At round end, if most of your lines bowed the same way, the drill names the tendency: a consistent curve belongs to the hand, not to the stroke. Running out of desk is not a fault: a stroke that stops before 88% of the way to B is not scored at all, and a circle at the lift point lets you carry the same one on.

What counts as straight

The measurement is RMS perpendicular drift away from the straight A-to-B path, in pixels, graded in a band: under max(0.4% × |AB|, 3px) is a clean 100, and the score reaches zero at ease × max(5.5% × |AB|, 16px). Up to 20 points come off for stopping short of B, and the round is the mean of the six. Ease is 1.0 for a pen, 1.5 for a finger and 2.0 for a mouse or trackpad, because a wrist-pivoted device cannot creep the way a nib can: the same 15px of wobble across a 300px pull is a fair mouse line and a careless pen one. None of it involves AI: the score is a closed-form calculation over the samples of your own stroke.
