$ man ellipse-in-plane

Draw mode (the studio way): sweep the whole ellipse onto the highlighted face, all the way round. Lifting does not end it — press again near where you stopped and the halves count as one sweep, which is what a trackpad needs. Your ink is fitted to an ellipse, then the handles appear so you can nudge it — or press R and draw it again. Handles mode skips the sweeping entirely: the centre moves the plate, the two rim dots set length + tilt, the small accent dot sets width. Same drill, same scoring — the button switches at any time.

Scored against the exact circle that face holds — its image is solved in closed form from the camera, not sketched in. Placement, tilt, size and how open the ellipse is (its degree) each count; nail them and it reads 100. The free zone is set for the hardware you are on — the HUD says which — and it never falls below a few real pixels, so a small phone sheet is not judged more harshly than a desktop one. Three faces per round, each turned further away than the last (your very first round is two, so the drill gives you a score before it gives you the hardest face), and after each lock the hint names whichever thing you missed by most.

Keys: arrows move · [ ] tilt · - = length · , . width · R start the plate over · Enter lock in, then next face, then new round. Hold shift for bigger steps.

The dashed line across the sheet is the horizon — your eye level. The dashed line through the reveal is the axle: the line straight out of the face, drawn for real and labelled on the sheet. The minor axis is the short way across an ellipse, the major the long way. An ellipse's degree is what templates are sold in — 30° means the short way across is sin 30° = half the long way.

Studio tip: the minor axis runs down the axle — on a wheel, straight out along the hub. Nail that direction before you touch the size.

Fitting an ellipse to a face seen at an angle

A box in two-point perspective arrives with one face highlighted, and you lay the ellipse belonging to that face onto it. Sweep the whole way round; lifting does not end the attempt, since a press near where you stopped joins the pieces — no trackpad crosses a 250px closed arc in one contact. The stroke is fitted, and handles appear to nudge centre, length, tilt and width; handles mode skips the sweep. Three faces per round, each turned further away.

This is the plate-on-a-table, wheel-on-an-axle problem: a circle only reads as lying on a surface when its ellipse belongs to that surface. The reveal draws the axle — the face’s real 3D normal projected through the same camera, not your minor axis extended — because the minor axis runs down the axle, straight out along the hub of a wheel. It prints the true ellipse’s degree — the number templates are sold in — and names whichever of placement, tilt or size you missed by most, so you leave with a direction, not just a mark.

What a locked plate is marked on

The truth is not sampled and fitted: the face’s inscribed circle lives in 3D and its conic is pushed straight through the plane-to-screen homography, so the revealed ellipse is that circle’s image to floating-point precision. Your attempt is scored on three errors: centre offset, normalized by the face’s mean projected diagonal; axis angle, which counts for less the rounder the truth is, since a near-circle points nowhere in particular; and size, which is scale plus the error in the degree itself, capped so it cannot zero an otherwise sound ellipse. Anything inside the full-credit zone reads 100; that zone is 2% of the face diagonal on a pen, doubled on a trackpad, and floored at five eased pixels, since 2% of a 150px phone face is smaller than a fingertip’s jitter. No model reads the sweep: your ellipse is reduced to a centre, two axes and a tilt, and those numbers are compared with the true ones.
